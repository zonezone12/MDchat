Self-assembled molecular cages hold guests in a cavity whose shape is set by noncovalent contacts between their building blocks [REF: review of self-assembled cages/capsules]. Because those contacts are weak, the cage does not sit in one structure. It breathes, opens and closes at individual contacts, and sometimes distorts as a whole, and these motions decide whether and how a guest gets in [1, 2]. Gear-shaped amphiphile (GSA) nanocubes are a clear example. Six molecules assemble in water into a cube held together by cation–π stacking at the poles and by interlocking substituents around the equator [REF: Hiraoka nanocube papers], and the methyl groups at those positions tune how rigid the cube is [1, 2]. All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) can follow this flexibility directly, and a typical study now runs tens of independent replicas per system to sample it [1].

Turning such an ensemble into a picture of its conformational states is the harder step, and it usually starts from human intuition. The analyst chooses a few order parameters in advance, sets cutoffs on them, and labels every frame. Whatever the chosen coordinates cannot see, such as an unexpected intermediate, a local defect or a deformation in an orientation nobody looked for, is averaged out or missed [3]. For GSA nanocubes, Murata et al. classified frames into metastructures by whether pole cation–π contacts are open and whether an equatorial contact is elongated [1]. The classification reproduces a clear structure–property trend, but it shows the limits of any threshold scheme. Frames near a cutoff flicker between labels, and combinations the scheme did not anticipate fall into an unassigned class, which held almost half of the frames for the least substituted cube [1]. The authors describe the metastructures as operational classes rather than thermodynamic states, and name more systematic, data-driven identification, for example by machine-learning clustering, as an important next step [1].

Data-driven alternatives exist, but none fits this setting directly. Unsupervised learning can compress and cluster simulation data without prior labels [4], and agnostic descriptors have been used to recognize molecular motifs without chemical intuition [3], to track defects in supramolecular polymers [5], to reconstruct motifs in dynamic micelles [6], and to detect local fluctuations and dynamic domains in self-assembling systems [7]. Machine-learned collective variables extract slow coordinates from trajectory pools instead of guessing them [8, 9], and deep learning has uncovered assembly pathways in colloidal mixtures [10]. Markov state models turn many short trajectories into kinetics [11, 12] and have been applied to self-assembly [13], but they need a lag time at which transitions are memoryless; for nanocubes, deformation and guest uptake overlap in time within 5 ns runs, and Murata et al. did not apply kinetic models for that reason [1]. Coarse-grained transition networks of nanocube assembly [14] address how six monomers come together, not how an assembled cube deforms. Most of these methods label local environments or single frames. An ensemble of replicas of one assembled cage poses a different set of questions: where in time each replica changes state, how many states the ensemble actually supports, which states are the same deformation seen in another orientation, which contacts define each state, and which replicas do not behave like the others.

Answering those questions is also slow. Mature toolkits such as MDAnalysis [15], MDTraj [16] and freud [17] compute coordinates, distances and order parameters efficiently, and newer tools wrap common analyses into single automated runs [18]. What they leave to the analyst are the decisions: which penalty or cutoff to use, how many clusters to keep, which clusters are artifacts of one trajectory, and which contacts to report. Those decisions are usually made by inspecting plots replica by replica, which costs time and is hard to reproduce. An analysis that makes these decisions from the data, and records how it made them, would both remove human bias and shorten the path from trajectories to interpretation.

Here we present such a workflow. It treats each replica as a multivariate time series of inter-monomer contact distances. Changepoint detection [19, 20] first divides every replica into dwells, stretches in which those distances stay in one regime, with the detection penalty set from the elbow of each ensemble's event-count curve rather than fixed by hand. Hierarchical clustering [21] then groups the dwells of all replicas into candidate states. Instead of fixing the number of states, the workflow screens a range of cuts. A cut is kept only if its clusters differ in identifiable contacts and recur across replicas, and among those cuts the smallest is chosen whose states predict cage geometry withheld from the clustering, on replicas left out of the fit, within error of the best cut. Because a symmetric cage can deform in several equivalent orientations, clusters that are the same deformation in different orientations are recognized by comparing their contact profiles under the cage's symmetry operations, and merged. Permutation-calibrated effect sizes with false-discovery control [22] then name the contacts that distinguish each state. The same screen also points the analyst to replicas that behave unlike the rest of the ensemble, which reduces the manual inspection that usually precedes any interpretation. No step uses reference labels or structural classification thresholds; published classifications are used only afterwards, to read the states.

We apply the workflow to six GSA nanocubes that differ in equatorial and pole methylation, 50 all-atom replicas each. Three of them are the systems classified by Murata et al. [1], which lets the unsupervised states be compared with an established threshold analysis of the same trajectories. The other three are pole-hydrogen variants reported here for the first time, which extend the comparison to a second substitution series. The paper is organized as follows. Methods describes the features, the segmentation, the screen over cuts, the symmetry merge and the contact attribution. Results first checks the workflow against the published classification, then describes the states and contacts it finds in each cube and the replicas it flags. The Discussion considers what the workflow assumes, where it may be applied to other flexible assemblies, and its limits.
